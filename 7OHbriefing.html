7-Hydroxymitragynine (7-OH): Regulatory & Public Health Brief

Prepared for legislators, regulators, and policy staff

7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) is the primary opioid-active compound responsible for kratom’s effects. While concentrated 7-OH products only began appearing widely in U.S. retail markets in 2024, the underlying biology is not new: the human body converts mitragynine (from kratom) into 7-OH. This metabolic process explains kratom’s opioid-like effects, dependence, and withdrawal.

Recent claims that “7-OH is dangerous but kratom is safe” misrepresent basic pharmacology. Kratom’s psychoactive and addictive effects depend on the formation of 7-OH in vivo. New 7-OH products eliminate this biological “speed bump” by delivering the active opioid compound directly.

Executive Summary Kratom Regulatory Brief FDA Position

Executive Summary

7-OH is the opioid-active driver. The pharmacologic effects attributed to kratom depend on metabolic conversion of mitragynine into 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), a potent μ-opioid receptor agonist.

New products, same biology. Concentrated 7-OH tablets, gummies, and shots entered U.S. retail markets broadly in 2024. These products bypass metabolism and deliver the active opioid compound directly.

False separation narrative. Claims that “7-OH causes harm but kratom does not” ignore that kratom’s effects require in-body formation of 7-OH. The distinction is commercial, not pharmacologic.

Regulatory implications. Both kratom and 7-OH products raise the same public-health concerns: dependence, withdrawal, overdose risk, youth access, and unapproved drug distribution.

Clarifying a Common Industry Claim

Some trade groups argue that concentrated 7-OH products are dangerous but that kratom leaf or powder is fundamentally different. This framing is misleading and inconsistent with established pharmacology.

Scientific reality: Mitragynine itself has weak opioid activity. Its clinical effects depend on conversion to 7-OH after ingestion.

Legislative analogy: This is analogous to arguing that heroin is dangerous but morphine is safe, despite heroin functioning as a prodrug.

What changed in 2024: Manufacturers began isolating and concentrating 7-OH into tablets and shots, removing the body’s natural metabolic limits.

Why this matters: The distinction affects enforcement strategy, not biological risk. Both pathways deliver the same opioid-active compound.

Clinical and Public Health Effects

Acute effects. Sedation, respiratory depression, impaired cognition, nausea, and loss of consciousness—particularly at higher doses or when combined with other depressants.

Dependence and withdrawal. Regular exposure to 7-OH produces tolerance and withdrawal syndromes clinically indistinguishable from opioid withdrawal.

Emergency care utilization. Poison control data and hospital reports describe increasing calls and ED visits associated with kratom and 7-OH use.

Vulnerable populations. Adolescents, pregnant individuals, and persons with prior substance use disorders face disproportionate risk.

Why Concentrated 7-OH Products Increase Risk

Traditional kratom products require metabolic conversion of mitragynine to 7-OH, introducing biological limits on dose and speed of onset. Concentrated 7-OH products remove these constraints.

Loss of metabolic “buffer.” Direct 7-OH delivery bypasses first-pass metabolism, increasing peak opioid exposure.

Faster onset. Tablets, gummies, and liquid shots deliver the active compound rapidly, increasing abuse liability.

Dosing uncertainty. Label variability and lack of pharmaceutical standards amplify overdose risk.

Regulatory significance. These features align 7-OH products with unapproved opioid drugs rather than dietary supplements.

Policy and Legislative Considerations

Clarify statutory treatment. Concentrated 7-OH products function as opioid-active drugs and may meet existing definitions of an unapproved drug, adulterated food, or controlled substance under state law.

Do not rely on artificial distinctions. Separating “7-OH products” from “kratom products” ignores the shared biological mechanism and invites regulatory loopholes rather than meaningful public-health protection.

Retail access restrictions. Removal from gas stations, vape shops, and convenience stores aligns enforcement with the opioid-like risk profile of these products.

Prevent downstream costs. Emergency care, neonatal withdrawal, addiction treatment, and enforcement burdens are shifted to states when opioid-active products remain broadly available.

Common Claims vs. Regulatory Reality

Claim: “Only isolated 7-OH products are dangerous.”
Reality: Kratom’s effects depend on metabolic conversion to 7-OH. The distinction is commercial, not pharmacologic.

Claim: “Kratom is safer because the body controls conversion.”
Reality: Metabolism limits dose speed, not dependence or withdrawal. Concentrated 7-OH products remove this limit entirely.

Claim: “Natural origin implies reduced risk.”
Reality: Botanical source does not change opioid receptor activity or abuse liability.

Claim: “Retail availability implies regulatory approval.”
Reality: Neither kratom nor 7-OH is FDA-approved; enforcement gaps do not confer safety or legality.
