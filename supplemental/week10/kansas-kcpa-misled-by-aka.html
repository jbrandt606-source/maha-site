Supplemental Reading Week 10 Special Kansas Evidence Review

How the American Kratom Association Misled Kansas to Pass the KCPA

When Kansas lawmakers were asked to pass the Kratom Consumer Protection Act (KCPA), they were told a reassuring story: that kratom is not an opioid, not addictive, and safe enough to regulate rather than restrict.

That story was built on selective citations, incomplete science, and critical omissions—many of them drawn from the very exhibits submitted as “evidence.”

Exhibit A: “Kratom Science Update – Evidence-Based Facts” (AKA / Pinney Associates)

What Kansas was told:
This document was presented as a neutral, science-based overview demonstrating low abuse potential and safe regulation.

What it actually is:
A paid advocacy document prepared for the American Kratom Association—treated as independent authority anyway.

What the document quietly admits (and why it matters):

  • Human metabolism: mitragynine is converted in the human body into 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH).
  • Potency: 7-OH is far more potent and opioid-active at the μ-opioid receptor than mitragynine.
  • Market manipulation: manufacturers have intentionally increased 7-OH concentrations in products.
  • Dependence: chronic use can cause dependence and withdrawal.
  • Adulteration: products have been found adulterated with opioids.

What Kansas lawmakers were not told:

  • Once you admit predictable human conversion to a more potent opioid, you cannot keep selling the fiction that this is “mitragynine alone” or “non-opioid.”
  • The document describes the very abuse mechanisms the KCPA claims to prevent, then asks lawmakers to accept labeling as the solution.

Why this supports Schedule I logic:

  • High abuse potential (potent opioid metabolite + product boosting).
  • No accepted medical use (no FDA approval; self-reported benefits are not medical use).
  • No accepted safety (dependence, withdrawal, adulteration).

Exhibit B: Systematic Review of Overdose Risk (Stanciu et al.)

What Kansas was told:
Most deaths involve other substances; therefore kratom itself is relatively safe.

What the review actually shows:

  • Exposure associated with seizures, respiratory depression, coma, intubation, and cardiac/respiratory arrest.
  • Naloxone responsiveness in at least one case—consistent with opioid-like toxicity.
  • Extracts/isolates/concentrates pose substantially higher risk than traditional preparations.
  • Alkaloids inhibit key liver enzymes, increasing serious drug–drug interaction risk.

What Kansas lawmakers were not told:

  • “Polysubstance” is not exculpatory; it describes predictable opioid-style interaction risk in the real world.
  • The paper warns against extrapolating traditional use to the modern retail market.

Why this supports Schedule I logic:

  • Lack of accepted safety under medical supervision.
  • Opioid-like overdose physiology and interactions.
  • Product variability incompatible with consumer retail sales.

Exhibit C: Abuse Liability Study of 7-Hydroxymitragynine (Hemby et al.)

What Kansas was told:
Low abuse potential based on animal studies.

What the study actually shows:

  • 7-OH is self-administered by animals, indicating reinforcing (addictive) properties.
  • 7-OH substitutes for morphine in abuse-liability models.
  • Prior exposure to 7-OH increases later morphine intake.

What Kansas lawmakers were not told:

  • This identifies the most dangerous component—not a harmless one.
  • 7-OH is not hypothetical: it is produced in humans and appears in products.

Why this supports Schedule I logic:

  • Clear evidence of high abuse potential.
  • Escalation risk toward other opioids.
  • No accepted medical use.

Exhibit D: “Kratom Tea as a Therapeutic Option” (Wilson et al.)

What Kansas was told:
Kratom may help with opioid dependence and shows limited respiratory risk.

What the study actually shows:

  • Even tea preparations produce μ-opioid receptor agonism.
  • Respiratory suppression was observed.
  • The authors acknowledge mitragynine’s conversion to 7-OH.
  • Human safety data are absent, not established.

What Kansas lawmakers were not told:

  • This is a controlled tea preparation—not modern retail extracts and enhanced products.
  • Calling something “therapeutic” without FDA approval does not establish accepted medical use.

Why this supports Schedule I logic:

  • Confirms opioid pharmacology.
  • Demonstrates physiologic risk.
  • Reinforces lack of accepted medical use.

Exhibit E: Mitragynine Self-Administration in Rats (Yue & Katz)

What Kansas was told:
Mitragynine is not reinforcing in animals, proving low abuse potential.

What the study actually shows:

  • Mitragynine binds to opioid receptors.
  • It alters opioid-seeking behavior.
  • It was tested in isolation, without accounting for human metabolism.

Critical Metabolism Omission (the problem that breaks the animal argument)

Rodents do not metabolize mitragynine into 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) in the same way humans do. That means animal-only conclusions about “mitragynine’s abuse potential” systematically underestimate real-world human risk, because humans are exposed to a more potent opioid agonist after dosing.

This is basic pharmacology. We do not assess a prodrug’s human risk in an animal model that cannot generate its active metabolite. Yet that is exactly the move being made here—without disclosure to lawmakers.

Why this supports Schedule I logic:

  • Animal models used to claim “low abuse potential” are not metabolically relevant.
  • Human risk is driven by a potent opioid metabolite.

Exhibit F: The KCPA Advocacy Deck

What Kansas was told:
The KCPA solves the problem through regulation.

What the deck itself admits:

  • Products must limit 7-OH.
  • Synthetic alkaloids must be banned.
  • Adulteration is a known problem.
  • Youth access is a concern.

What this really means:

If a substance requires potency caps, metabolite limits, bans on chemical manipulation, and strict age controls, lawmakers are not treating it like a supplement. They are treating it like a drug.

Why this supports Schedule I logic:

  • The market predictably drifts toward higher opioid potency.
  • Consumer regulation cannot fix a pharmacologic problem.

Final Synthesis: Kansas Was Given the Case for Schedule I—Not the KCPA

From the materials submitted to Kansas:

  • Kratom’s effects are mediated by opioid receptor agonism.
  • Humans are exposed to 7-hydroxymitragynine, a more potent opioid compound produced after dosing.
  • That compound shows clear abuse liability.
  • Overdose physiology, respiratory depression, seizures, and interactions are documented.
  • There is no accepted medical use.
  • Safety claims rely on animal models that do not reproduce human metabolic conversion.

Kansas lawmakers were not given neutral science. They were given a curated narrative—one that collapses under its own citations.

The evidence used to justify the KCPA actually satisfies the core criteria for Schedule I placement.
