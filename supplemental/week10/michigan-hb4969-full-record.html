Michigan Lawmakers Deserve the Whole Record on HB 4969

On October 30, 2025, the Michigan House Committee on Regulatory Reform received testimony in support of HB 4969. The presentation was polished, citation-heavy, and framed as science-driven.

But when you slow it down and compare it to the underlying federal record, the picture becomes more complicated.

The Framing Presented to Michigan

The testimony presented several core claims:

  • The FDA has shifted its position.
  • Natural leaf is distinct from 7-hydroxymitragynine products.
  • A small FDA human study demonstrates tolerability.
  • Millions use kratom safely.
  • Federal posture supports incremental regulation.

Those claims deserve to be measured against the full federal record, not headlines.

The “FDA Changed Its Position” Claim

The testimony suggests that the FDA dramatically softened its stance and is no longer focused on natural leaf products. That framing implies a safety shift.

What did not change:

  • The FDA has not approved kratom for any medical use.
  • The FDA has not declared kratom safe.
  • The FDA has not issued a formal safety determination.
  • The FDA continues to warn of serious adverse events.

Enforcement emphasis is not endorsement. Regulatory posture is not regulatory approval.

The Single Ascending Dose Study

Michigan legislators were told that a small FDA Single Ascending Dose study showed kratom was “well tolerated.”

Important context:

  • Approximately 40 participants.
  • Short-term exposure.
  • Acute tolerability endpoints only.
  • No long-term follow-up.
  • No dependency measurement.
  • No chronic-use modeling.

Short-term tolerability is not long-term safety. It does not measure addiction liability. It does not measure withdrawal.

Critical context: The same federal research pathway includes a Human Abuse Potential study. Abuse potential studies are not conducted on substances considered benign.

The 7-Hydroxymitragynine Line in the Sand

The testimony draws a bright line between “natural leaf” and 7-hydroxymitragynine products.

That distinction is rhetorically useful. It is pharmacologically incomplete.

  • Mitragynine is the primary alkaloid in kratom leaf.
  • In humans, mitragynine is metabolized into 7-hydroxymitragynine.
  • 7-hydroxymitragynine is the more potent mu-opioid receptor agonist.
Biology does not respect marketing categories.
Even when a product is labeled “natural leaf,” the human body converts mitragynine into a more potent opioid compound.

The metabolic pathway is not theoretical. It is acknowledged in federal materials and scientific literature.

Why Animal Studies Cannot Resolve Human Risk

Some arguments rely on animal self-administration models to claim low abuse potential.

What is rarely explained:

  • Rodents do not metabolize mitragynine into 7-hydroxymitragynine in the same way humans do.
  • Human exposure includes the downstream opioid metabolite.
  • Animal models therefore underestimate human opioid exposure.

We do not assess codeine safety in animals that cannot convert it into morphine. The same principle applies here.

The Federal Court Narrative

The testimony references a federal case and suggests the FDA “refused” to defend its position or has not determined whether kratom is dangerous.

The case described involved falsified shipping manifests and resulted in a guilty plea. It was not:

  • A scientific evidentiary trial on kratom safety
  • A federal rulemaking
  • A judicial determination that kratom is safe

Sentencing hearings are not where national toxicology standards are decided.

Framing a criminal proceeding as a scientific exoneration is a rhetorical maneuver — not a regulatory reversal.

“Kratom Can Be Lawfully Marketed as Food”

The testimony argues kratom may be lawfully marketed as food because foods do not require FDA preapproval.

Technically true. Strategically incomplete.

  • The FDA has repeatedly taken enforcement action against kratom products.
  • Kratom has not been recognized as a lawful dietary ingredient.
  • The FDA has not issued a safety determination under the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act.

The absence of preapproval requirements does not equal blanket legality.

Regulatory nuance was presented as broad authorization. That distinction matters for Michigan policymakers.

The NIDA and Research Framing

The testimony cites research commentary suggesting kratom has “interesting properties” and warrants further study.

Federal research funding does not equal:

  • Safety endorsement
  • Clinical approval
  • Policy validation

Scientific curiosity and regulatory approval are not interchangeable concepts.

“Millions Use It Safely”

The testimony references millions of users.

Prevalence is not proof of safety.

  • Millions used synthetic cannabinoids before widespread regulation.
  • Millions used phenibut before enforcement action.
  • Millions used tianeptine before scheduling debates.

Widespread use measures access — not toxicology.

Congressional Appropriations Language

The testimony cites appropriations report language suggesting Congress rejected scheduling.

Appropriations report language:

  • Is not statutory law
  • Does not bind FDA or DEA authority
  • Does not override state regulatory authority

It reflects a funding posture — not a scientific determination.

The 7-Hydroxymitragynine Line in the Sand

The testimony draws a sharp distinction between “natural leaf” and 7-hydroxymitragynine products.

That distinction is politically useful. It is pharmacologically incomplete.

Mitragynine, the primary alkaloid in kratom leaf, is metabolized in the human body into 7-hydroxymitragynine.

That metabolite is the more potent mu-opioid receptor agonist.

Pharmacology does not respect marketing categories. Biology does not change because a product is labeled “natural.”

Regulating concentrated 7-OH products may be appropriate. But the existence of a stronger opioid metabolite produced in the human body cannot be removed from the policy discussion.

What Michigan Should Actually Be Asking

Before accepting the framing presented in support of HB 4969, three questions matter:

  1. Has the FDA issued a formal safety determination on kratom?
    No.
  2. Has dependency risk been ruled out?
    No. A Human Abuse Potential study is still underway.
  3. Does short-term tolerability equal long-term safety?
    No.

The Larger Issue

HB 4969 is not about regulating spinach.

It concerns a product that:

  • Acts on opioid receptors
  • Produces a more potent opioid metabolite inside the body
  • Is under ongoing federal abuse-potential evaluation
  • Has no FDA-approved medical use

Michigan lawmakers may choose regulation, scheduling, or incremental control.

But whatever the decision, it should be grounded in the full regulatory record — not selective characterization of federal posture or preliminary research.

When policy is built on incomplete framing, it does not age well.

Michigan deserves precision.
