The Sponsored Trip: How Industry Paid for Influence Abroad

Source note: This section is based entirely on FOIA-released emails and attachments linked below. The original PDFs are provided in full so readers can independently verify dates, logistics, and context.

Open: AKA Indonesia trip (PDF) → Open: Aka Indonesia trip 2 (PDF) → Open: Aka DC slides (PDF) →

The email trail surrounding the Indonesia trip removes any ambiguity about what this was. This was not an independent academic exchange. It was a trade association–funded advocacy mission organized to influence foreign drug policy at a moment of active regulatory risk.

At the time of this trip, Indonesia was openly considering restrictions on kratom export. That context matters. The country supplies the overwhelming majority of the global market. Any policy shift threatened not only commercial access, but the industry’s long-term survival.

Against that backdrop, the American Kratom Association did not merely facilitate introductions. It financed, choreographed, and branded the entire engagement.

What the documents show

The FOIA record shows AKA staff coordinating:

  • International airfare and itineraries for U.S.-based researchers
  • Hotel accommodations at a major Jakarta hotel
  • Airport transfers and internal travel logistics
  • Scheduled dinners and site visits
  • Farm tours framed as supply-chain “education”

This is not how independent science is shared. This is how lobbying campaigns are run — with comfort, coordination, and message control.

Public funding, private delivery

Several of the researchers participating in these meetings have conducted work supported by NIH and NIDA. Publicly funded science carries an expectation of independence. That expectation collapses when an interested trade association pays for travel, lodging, and logistical support to place those researchers in front of policymakers.

When industry covers the cost, controls the setting, and manages the schedule, the resulting presentation is no longer neutral. It is advocacy — delivered with citations.

When lobbying starts to look like branding

One internal email captures the nature of this trip more clearly than any policy memo ever could:

“The AKA is designing a polo shirt for the kratom stakeholders to wear in Indonesia… Could you please provide your shirt size?”
— AKA internal email (emphasis added)

This is not a scientific conference. It is not a diplomatic exchange. It is a coordinated messaging operation complete with branded apparel.

Reducing a foreign drug-policy engagement to matching polo shirts strips away any remaining pretense of independence. It reveals a campaign mindset: uniform appearance, disciplined messaging, and a curated group presented as authoritative.

Why this crosses a line

Paid international travel for scientists and policy influencers creates an unavoidable conflict of interest. It blurs the line between research and lobbying. It substitutes financial sponsorship for institutional legitimacy.

More troubling, it injects private commercial interests directly into foreign policy decisions, bypassing normal diplomatic, regulatory, and academic channels.

Indonesian officials were not simply hearing “what the science says.” They were hearing what industry chose to sponsor, stage, and emphasize — at a time when export restrictions were actively under consideration.

The paper trail surrounding the Congressional briefing shows not a spontaneous exchange of ideas, but a fully managed advocacy event—planned, financed, and executed by an industry trade association. From travel to talking points, the briefing was orchestrated end to end.

How the event was assembled

The FOIA record documents a step-by-step coordination timeline handled by the American Kratom Association:

  • Speaker lineup selection and sequencing
  • Slide deck collection, editing, and advance submission
  • Venue coordination inside a Congressional office building
  • Hotel reservations and room blocks
  • Van pickups and ground transportation
  • Breakfast and meeting logistics
  • Travel booking and reimbursement instructions

These are not the mechanics of an independent scientific symposium. They are the mechanics of a business-sponsored advocacy briefing.

Why the venue matters

Holding the event inside a Congressional building conferred an aura of official legitimacy. To staffers and Members walking by, the setting implies neutrality, credibility, and institutional endorsement.

That legitimacy was not earned through independent sponsorship or governmental review. It was rented—using industry coordination, industry logistics, and industry reimbursement.

When reimbursement replaces independence

One line in the email chain collapses any remaining pretense that this was a neutral briefing:

“Please send travel receipts as soon as possible to our CFO, Tom Moore…”
— AKA internal email (emphasis added)

That sentence transforms a supposed scientific briefing into a lobby expense report. It establishes, in plain language, that travel costs were treated as reimbursable business expenses overseen by the trade association’s chief financial officer.

When a CFO is processing receipts for scientists appearing in a Congressional building, the line between research and lobbying is not blurred—it is erased.

Public funding, private choreography

Portions of the research highlighted during the briefing cite NIH and NIDA support. Public funding carries an expectation of independence in presentation and interpretation.

That expectation does not survive when an interested trade group:

  • selects the speakers,
  • controls the slides,
  • books the rooms,
  • arranges transportation, and
  • reimburses travel through its CFO.

What Congress received was not detached science. It was curated messaging delivered with the optics of authority and the logistics of a corporate event.

Why this matters

Congressional briefings influence enforcement priorities, legislative timelines, and the willingness to tolerate regulatory delay. When those briefings are industry-built and industry-paid, policymakers are not being informed—they are being managed.

The Arkansas interim committee hearing was presented publicly as an opportunity for lawmakers to hear “expert education.” The internal record shows something else entirely: a hearing engineered by a trade association to deliver a coordinated message with the appearance of independent testimony.

How the testimony was managed

The FOIA emails document a tightly controlled plan:

  • A scripted order of witnesses designed to frame the discussion
  • Advance collection and review of slides and talking points
  • Managed travel schedules to ensure coordinated arrivals and departures
  • Hotel rooms booked to keep participants centralized and available
  • A private dinner with legislators immediately before testimony

These are not the hallmarks of neutral expert participation. They are the mechanics of a lobbying operation.

The quote that collapses the narrative

One line in the planning emails captures the reality of the event with brutal clarity:

“AKA will make hotel arrangements… Dinner meeting with Committee members.”
— AKA internal email (emphasis added)

That sentence alone defeats the claim of independence. When a trade association is arranging lodging and scheduling private meals with legislators in advance of testimony, the resulting appearance is not expert education. It is managed influence.

Why the order matters

The emails also show an explicit effort to control witness sequencing— ensuring that the “scientist panel” spoke first. This tactic sets the frame, establishes credibility early, and makes subsequent opposition sound reactive rather than substantive.

That is a known advocacy strategy. It is not an accident of scheduling.

Logistics as leverage

Travel coordination, hotel bookings, and pre-hearing dinners are not incidental conveniences. They create dependency, cohesion, and message discipline. They also signal to lawmakers that the speakers arrive as a unified bloc, implicitly endorsed by the organization managing the event.

When logistics are handled by the lobby, testimony is no longer independent—even if the words spoken are factual. Context matters, and context here was manufactured.

Why this matters for policymakers

Legislators rely on hearings to distinguish evidence from advocacy. When a hearing is built, staffed, and staged by an interested trade group, that distinction collapses.

What Arkansas received was not a spontaneous presentation of expert opinion. It was a coordinated campaign, delivered on a schedule, over dinner, and underwritten by the lobby itself.

The 7-OH Statement: A Defense That Fails Its Own Timeline

Source note: This section is based entirely on the June 2024 written statement linked below. Readers are encouraged to review the full document to verify authorship, language, and timing.

Open: AKA 7-OH Statement (PDF) →

In June 2024, a group of researchers issued a statement attempting to draw a sharp boundary between so-called “traditional” kratom and products containing elevated levels of 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.

The authors argue that these high-potency products “should not be scientifically considered or commercially categorized as kratom products,” and describe them as having had a “recent appearance” in the marketplace.

That framing is not merely convenient. It is fatal to the defense.

The timeline problem the statement cannot escape

By the authors’ own admission, products containing elevated 7-OH and pseudoindoxyl are a recent market development. That matters because the documented harms attributed to kratom are not recent.

FDA adverse-event reports, CDC SUDORS data, poison control records, and published case reports documenting overdoses, dependence, withdrawal, and deaths span many years prior to 2024.

Those injuries and fatalities occurred before the authors now claim these newer products entered the market.

The defense collapses under its own logic

If high-7-OH and pseudoindoxyl products are truly a recent phenomenon, then they cannot plausibly explain the long-standing body of harm that triggered regulatory concern in the first place.

And yet, this statement is now being used by industry advocates to retroactively shift responsibility onto compounds that were not present when most documented harms occurred.

That is not clarification. It is historical revision.

The most damaging admissions — in their own words

“Such products should not be scientifically considered or commercially categorized as kratom products.”
“The recent appearance of 7-OH mitragynine and mitragynine pseudoindoxyl products…”
— June 2024 7-OH Statement (emphasis added)

Read together, these lines do not exonerate kratom. They do the opposite.

They confirm that:

  • Regulatory concern predates the emergence of these newer formulations
  • The market was already producing injury, dependence, and death
  • The harms were attributed to products sold and consumed as kratom

The statement inadvertently admits that the danger existed before the compounds now being singled out as scapegoats.

A post-hoc blame shift, not a scientific defense

What this document represents is not a careful parsing of risk. It is a late-stage distancing strategy: an attempt to preserve the broader category by isolating responsibility after the damage has already been done.

Science does not work backward from political need. Public health does not get rewritten because a market evolves.

The harms were real. The products were real. The timeline is real.

When a defense depends on pretending yesterday’s injuries were caused by today’s compounds, it is not a defense at all. It is desperation.

Readers are encouraged to open the PDF above and review the language directly. The contradiction is not inferred. It is stated plainly once the calendar is taken seriously.

The Drafting Trail: Lobby Edits, Scientific Silence

Source note: This section is based entirely on FOIA-released correspondence linked below. The original PDF is provided so readers can independently verify dates, participants, and language.

Open: AKA Statement Drafting Emails (PDF) →

This is not a case of lawmakers being influenced by industry. It is something more troubling: industry editing the language of science itself, with the knowledge and participation of respected academic researchers.

In April 2024, JW Ross, a senior strategist with the Global Kratom Coalition, inserted himself directly into the drafting of a “science statement” intended for submission to a legislative health committee. The recipients of that email included University of Florida professors Dr. Oliver Grundmann and Dr. Christopher McCurdy, along with other pro-kratom scientists.

The edit request that exposes the intent

Ross objected to a line in the draft stating that certain formulations “may confer a higher risk profile.” His concern was not scientific uncertainty. It was political utility.

“If this is your position then I would suggest that ‘may’ be changed to ‘would’ so that it’s clear that that’s your intent. The reason this is so important is that if we have ambiguity, it’s fertile ground for those who want to ban all kratom.”
— JW Ross, April 2, 2024 (emphasis added)

That sentence strips away any remaining pretense of neutral inquiry. This was not a discussion about accuracy, data strength, or evidentiary thresholds. It was a discussion about how to shape regulatory outcomes.

This was not peer review — it was strategy

Ross is not a scientist. He does not conduct toxicology, pharmacology, or clinical research. He is a lobbyist.

And yet here he is, line-editing the language of a scientific document that would later be cited to legislators as expert consensus.

The purpose of the requested change was explicit: to eliminate ambiguity that could justify regulation or prohibition. That is not how scientific caution works. It is how advocacy messaging is hardened for policy use.

The silence that matters

There is no evidence in the email chain that any academic in the thread objected to the premise of the request, rejected the framing, or distanced themselves from lobbyist-authored edits.

There is also no public disclosure, in subsequent testimony or submissions, that a lobbyist requested specific wording changes for strategic regulatory reasons.

That silence is not neutral. It allowed industry priorities to pass through scientific channels without attribution.

Why this undermines trust

Legislators rely on scientific statements because they assume the language reflects uncertainty, nuance, and restraint. When that language is negotiated with lobbyists to prevent regulatory action, the foundation collapses.

This is not how evidence-based policy is built. It is how narratives are engineered.

When a lobbyist is arguing over “may” versus “would,” the goal is not truth. It is control.

Readers are strongly encouraged to open the PDF above and read the email chain directly. The intent is not inferred. It is stated in plain language.

The GMP Illusion: How a Trade Group Borrowed FDA Language to Manufacture Legitimacy

Source note: This section is based entirely on the “GMP Standards Program 3.0” presentation linked below. Readers are encouraged to review the slides directly to verify scope, administration, and enforcement claims.

Open: AKA GMP Standards 3.0 (Slides 167–188) →

The American Kratom Association’s so-called “GMP Standards Program 3.0” is not merely inadequate. It is structurally deceptive.

The program borrows the language, formatting, and tone of FDA regulatory frameworks to manufacture the appearance of legitimacy for a product the FDA has repeatedly and unequivocally stated is not a lawful dietary supplement.

This is not a technical oversight. It is a strategy.

Borrowing FDA language without FDA authority

The slides repeatedly invoke dietary supplement GMP concepts drawn from 21 C.F.R. Part 111, inviting lawmakers to assume that a recognized regulatory baseline exists.

It does not.

The FDA has stated—across warning letters, import alerts, and public advisories— that kratom does not qualify as a lawful dietary supplement or food additive. Any program built on dietary supplement GMPs is therefore legally irrelevant from the outset.

The lobby presents this program as if it fills a regulatory void. In reality, it papers over it.

How the program misleads at every level

  • Lawmakers are led to believe a functioning, FDA-adjacent safety framework exists, making further oversight unnecessary.
  • Retailers are led to believe participation confers compliance or legal protection.
  • Consumers are led to believe “GMP-certified” language reflects meaningful safety vetting.

None of those beliefs are true.

The program is voluntary, administered by the trade group itself, and entirely devoid of enforcement authority.

What the program does not have

  • No independent regulator
  • No public audit reports
  • No transparent corrective actions
  • No penalties for failure
  • No mandatory recalls
  • No referral mechanism to FDA or state authorities

It is not regulation. It is self-attestation with a logo.

The slide that gives the game away

Among the listed “standards,” one instruction stands out:

“The label… shall not infer in any way the product will produce a ‘high’ to a consumer.”

This is not consumer protection. It is consumer concealment.

A program that must explicitly instruct sellers not to imply psychoactive effects is not addressing risk. It is managing optics.

The embarrassment is not the wording itself, but what it reveals: an acute awareness that these products do produce intoxication, and that acknowledging this truth would invite scrutiny the market cannot survive.

“Collaboration” as a substitute for consequences

Other slides emphasize a “collaborative,” “constructive,” and “phased” approach. In regulatory terms, these are euphemisms.

The program promises guidance, webinars, and model SOPs, but nowhere does it impose mandatory corrective action, product withdrawal, or exclusion from the market.

In short: nothing happens when a company fails.

Why this matters for public policy

This GMP program has been repeatedly cited in hearings and legislative debates as evidence that kratom can be “regulated responsibly.”

But regulation requires authority, independence, and enforcement. This program has none of the three.

By cloaking itself in FDA terminology while selling a product the FDA says is illegal, the kratom lobby is not filling a regulatory gap. It is exploiting one.

This is not regulation.
It is regulation theater.

And it fails precisely where real regulation begins: accountability.

Readers are encouraged to open the GMP slides above and read the scope and limitations directly. The absence of enforcement is stated plainly in the program’s own materials.

University Emails: The Admission That Collapses the Narrative

Source note: This section is based entirely on the University of Florida–related email chain linked below. Readers are encouraged to open the PDF and verify dates, participants, and language directly.

Open: University of Florida Aka 2-401 (PDF) →

This email thread is the internal narrative the lobby never wanted public.

On January 27, 2024, University of Florida pharmacologist Dr. Oliver Grundmann—frequently cited in policy hearings—wrote privately to trade-association leadership, extract vendors, and fellow researchers to explain what all parties already understood: extracts and concentrates are chemically distinct from leaf material and should be regulated separately.

“Extracts and concentrates do have a place and should be available to consumers. But they are also distinct in many regards from native kratom leaf material…”
— University of Florida email (January 27, 2024)

That statement alone contradicts years of public messaging that treats all products as interchangeable under a single, benign category.

An admission of uncertainty — in plain language

The same email goes further. It acknowledges chemical complexity and unknowns that directly undermine claims of predictability and safety:

“As Chris [McCurdy] likes to point out, the contribution of each alkaloid (and potentially other, yet unknown compounds) is like a symphony…”
— Same email thread

This is not a minor aside. It is an explicit admission of variability, interaction, and incomplete knowledge—coming from the very scientists presented publicly as authorities on consistency and control.

The public story vs. the private record

The contradiction is devastating:

  • In private, extracts and concentrates are acknowledged as distinct, chemically complex, and insufficiently studied.
  • In public, the lobby insists “kratom is kratom”— whether leaf powder, resin, vape extract, or concentrated shot.

The email record shows that this was not confusion. It was compartmentalization.

The moment of candor that ends the debate

In the same thread, JW Ross—a senior industry strategist—states the bleak reality outright:

“It’s just a matter of time till enough people get hurt by these products and all kratom gets banned.”
— JW Ross, same email thread

That sentence admits three things at once:

  • The current market is unsafe
  • Self-regulation is failing
  • Government intervention is inevitable once harm becomes undeniable

This is not a warning from critics. It is an internal acknowledgment from within the advocacy network itself.

Why this matters for lawmakers and regulators

You are being told that these products are “just a tea leaf.” Behind closed doors, the industry’s own experts describe a chemically complex mixture with unknown contributors to effect and risk.

When scientists privately acknowledge uncertainty and differentiation, while publicly allowing a one-size-fits-all defense, the integrity of the testimony collapses.

This email does not merely contradict the public narrative.
It proves the narrative was never complete.
