The Story of Thebaine and How It Relates to Kratom

Thebaine built fortunes because the liver can convert it into morphine and codeine. Kratom’s mitragynine pulls the same stunt—once it hits the liver, the chemistry finishes and out comes 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH). Different plants, same trick: let the body do the dirty work.

Thebaine: the flip from harmless to opioid

Thebaine starts as a poppy alkaloid that isn’t used directly as a medicine. Inside the liver, enzymes like CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 can reshape it through demethylation/reduction into morphine and codeine. You don’t just take opioids—you can make them, thanks to metabolism.

Kratom’s mirror move: mitragynine → 7-OH

Mitragynine, kratom’s headline alkaloid, isn’t the finish line. The liver—again starring CYP3A4—oxidizes it to 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH), a metabolite with potent μ-opioid receptor activity. Mitragynine is the fuse; 7-OH is the blast.

Parallel in one line

Thebaine → Morphine/Codeine.
Mitragynine → 7-Hydroxymitragynine.
The liver flips the switch in both cases.

Why Big Pharma won’t touch 7-OH

If 7-OH were viable, it’d already be in a blister pack. It isn’t. The reasons are blunt: erratic potency, ugly safety signals, person-to-person variability, and legal liability. Big Pharma made billions off thebaine’s controlled conversions—but they’re not suicidal enough to bankroll kratom’s metabolite.

Bottom line

Thebaine was profitable because its conversion could be controlled. Kratom’s conversion happens inside the user—with no oversight. Same playbook. Worse risks.
