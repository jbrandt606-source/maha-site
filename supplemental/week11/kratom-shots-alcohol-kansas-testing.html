The point is not that every product tested the same. The point is that multiple products did not. That is exactly what a reporter, regulator, or legislator should care about.

In March 2026, the Kansas Department of Revenue, through its Alcoholic Beverage Control Division, cut straight through the fantasy that kratom retail is being meaningfully regulated.

Kansas found that some kratom liquid products sold at retail contained enough ethanol to be treated as alcoholic beverages under state law.

That is not a labeling issue. It is not a branding issue. It is not a minor paperwork issue.

It is a classification problem.

What Kansas Found

According to KCTV5, Kansas ABC tested kratom liquid products and found that O.P.M.S. Black Kratom Extract and O.P.M.S. Gold Kratom Extract contained approximately 15.7% to 16.15% ethanol alcohol.

  • Products identified included O.P.M.S. Black and O.P.M.S. Gold liquid extracts
  • Reported ethanol levels were about 15.7% to 16.15%
  • Kansas executed search warrants at 12 businesses
  • A total of 1,006 bottles were seized
  • Enforcement was based on unlawful sale of alcoholic beverages by unlicensed businesses

Kansas did not need a new “kratom framework” to act. It used an existing one.

What Kansas Actually Proved

Kansas did not claim that all kratom products contain alcohol.

They demonstrated something more specific — and more consequential.

Certain products marketed as “kratom shots” were functioning as alcoholic beverages while being sold entirely outside the alcohol regulatory system.

  • Alcohol is regulated
  • Kratom is not clearly regulated
  • These products exist between those systems
Key point

A product can contain alcohol at beverage-level concentrations and still avoid alcohol labeling, taxation, and enforcement depending on how it is categorized.

This Is Not a One-Off

The Kansas action would be significant on its own.

What makes it more significant is that independent lab testing shows the same pattern across multiple products, manufacturers, and testing facilities.

This is where third-party data becomes critical.

What Independent Lab Testing Shows

Independent Certificates of Analysis across multiple laboratories show a consistent pattern: ethanol is present in kratom liquid products at levels ranging from near-threshold concentrations to levels comparable with alcoholic beverages.

Examples drawn from compiled reports:

  • K-OH MIT liquid shot — 446,000 µg/g ethanol (~44.6%) (FAIL)
  • Ultra-NanOH liquid extract — 455,000 µg/g ethanol (~45.5%) (FAIL)
  • OPMS Gold liquid shot — 336,000 µg/g ethanol (~33.6%) (FAIL)
  • OPMS Red liquid shot — 47,200 µg/g ethanol (~4.7%) (FAIL)
  • Kream Ohmz shot — 59,900 ppm ethanol (~6.0%) (FAIL)
  • MIT45 liquid gel — 15,500 ppm ethanol (~1.5%) (FAIL)
  • Feel Free liquid shot — 5,300–8,220 ppm ethanol (~0.5–0.8%) (FAIL)

Comparator threshold referenced in these reports: 5,000 ppm (0.5%).

Some products exceed that level by 10 times, 50 times, and in multiple cases more than 80 times.

These findings align with independent testing highlighted by TestMyKratom.org , which has documented variability and labeling gaps across retail products.

What these numbers are not

These are not trace solvent residues.
  • They are repeated across products and brands
  • They are confirmed across independent laboratories
  • They include concentrations consistent with alcoholic beverages in multiple cases

At these levels, the data is not consistent with incidental contamination.

It reflects product composition.

The Question States With “Consumer Protection” Laws Cannot Answer

Many states have already passed Kratom Consumer Protection Acts (KCPAs), framed as a way to regulate the market and protect consumers.

Those laws are often presented as proof that the problem is already being handled.

Kansas shows otherwise.

So the obvious question is:

Where were those protections when these products were being sold?

Products with measurable — and in some cases extreme — ethanol levels were available at retail before any enforcement action occurred.

Why didn’t those laws catch this?

Because nobody is testing.
Nobody is inspecting.
Nobody is enforcing.

Standards written into statute do not enforce themselves.

If a product is never verified, it is never regulated in practice.

This Is an Enforcement Problem, Not a Labeling Problem

This issue is often framed as a labeling gap or a manufacturing inconsistency. That framing minimizes what is actually happening.

The problem is that products can enter and remain in retail circulation without meaningful verification of what they contain.

  • No consistent pre-market verification is evident before products reach shelves
  • No routine shelf-level inspection system is catching these products in real time
  • No uniform classification system determines how products containing alcohol should be treated

That combination creates a predictable outcome:

Products are sold first. Composition is discovered later.

Practical reality

If a regulator cannot identify a violation at the shelf, the regulation cannot function as intended.

What Kansas Did Differently

Kansas did not rely on a new regulatory framework.

It applied an existing one.

By treating certain kratom liquid products as alcoholic beverages based on composition, the state enforced laws that were already in place.

That approach avoided the ambiguity that exists in other states.

It focused on what the product is — not how it is marketed.

Why This Matters

There are two separate issues now clearly visible:

  • Some kratom liquid products can contain alcohol at levels that trigger existing alcohol laws
  • States that claim to regulate these products are not consistently detecting or acting on that reality

That gap is not theoretical.

It is measurable.

And it is currently being filled by independent testing — not regulatory oversight.

What This Means Without Spin

These products are being sold:

  • Without alcohol labeling
  • Without alcohol taxation
  • Without alcohol licensure

Yet independent testing shows that some contain measurable — and in multiple cases significant — ethanol.

That is not a narrow compliance issue.

It is a structural gap between how products are sold and how they would be regulated if classified differently.

Reporter Takeaways

  • Kansas action: A state alcohol enforcement agency identified kratom liquid products with ethanol levels consistent with alcoholic beverages and seized them under existing alcohol law.
  • Independent data: Multiple Certificates of Analysis show ethanol present across products at levels ranging from near-threshold to far exceeding comparator limits.
  • Market reality: Product-to-product variability exists, including both passing and failing results, indicating inconsistent composition in the retail market.
  • Regulatory gap: States that have passed kratom “consumer protection” laws did not detect or act on these products before independent testing surfaced the issue.
  • Central question: If these laws are intended to protect consumers, why is independent testing — rather than routine enforcement — identifying the problem?

Bottom Line

Kansas showed that some kratom liquid products meet the definition of alcohol under state law.

Independent lab data shows that Kansas did not identify an isolated anomaly.

It identified a category problem.

Products are being sold in one regulatory category while functioning, in some cases, as something else entirely.

And in states that claim these products are already “regulated,” the same question remains:

Who is actually verifying what is being sold?

Right now, the answer appears to be:

Not the system that was supposed to.
