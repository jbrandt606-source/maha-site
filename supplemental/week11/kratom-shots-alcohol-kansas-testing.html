In March 2026, the Kansas Department of Revenue, through its Alcoholic Beverage Control Division, made a finding that cuts directly through the narrative surrounding kratom regulation.

Some kratom liquid products being sold in Kansas contained enough ethanol to meet the definition of alcohol under state law.

That is not a labeling issue. It is a classification problem.

What Kansas Found

According to reporting by KCTV5, the Kansas Alcoholic Beverage Control Division tested kratom liquid products and found ethanol levels consistent with alcoholic beverages.

  • Products tested included O.P.M.S. Black and O.P.M.S. Gold liquid extracts
  • Measured ethanol levels were approximately 15–16 percent
  • More than 1,000 bottles were seized across multiple retailers
  • Enforcement was based on unlicensed sale of alcohol, not supplement violations

Full source: KCTV5 News — March 13, 2026

What Kansas Actually Proved

Kansas did not claim that all kratom products contain alcohol.

They did something more precise.

They demonstrated that certain products marketed as “kratom shots” were functioning as alcoholic beverages outside the alcohol regulatory system.

  • Alcohol is regulated
  • Kratom is not clearly regulated
  • These products exist between those systems
Key point

A product can contain alcohol at beverage levels and still avoid alcohol labeling, alcohol taxation, and alcohol enforcement depending on how it is marketed.

This Is Not a One-Off

The Kansas action would matter on its own.

What makes it more significant is that independent lab testing shows the same pattern across multiple products.

This is where third-party testing becomes critical.

What Independent Lab Testing Shows

Independent Certificates of Analysis and third-party testing highlight a consistent pattern: ethanol is present at measurable, repeatable levels across multiple kratom liquid products.

Examples drawn from compiled reports:

  • MIT45 Black (liquid gel) — 15,500 ppm ethanol (FAIL)
  • OPMS Gold liquid shot — 336,000 µg/g ethanol (FAIL)
  • Feel Free liquid shot — 5,300 ppm ethanol (FAIL)
  • Liquid Kratom Extract (KK-PL125) — 21,990 µg/g ethanol (FAIL)
  • K-OH MIT liquid shot — 446,000 µg/g ethanol (FAIL)
  • Additional 2026 lot — 15,600 ppm ethanol (FAIL)

These findings are consistent with independent testing highlighted by TestMyKratom, whose work has helped surface product variability and labeling gaps across the retail market.

What these numbers are not

These are not trace solvent residues.
  • They are repeated across products
  • They are quantifiable across labs
  • They exceed thresholds commonly used in USP <467> frameworks

In other words, this is not incidental contamination. It is product composition.

What This Means Without Spin

These products are being sold:

  • Without alcohol labeling
  • Without alcohol taxation
  • Without alcohol enforcement

Yet they may contain significant ethanol.

That is not a technical violation. It is a structural gap.

The Regulatory Gap

Right now, these products exist in a space where:

  • They are not approved drugs
  • They are not lawful dietary supplements under FDA position
  • They are not regulated as alcohol

But they may function like alcohol in composition.

Key observation

A product can contain alcohol but avoid alcohol law if it is sold under a different category.

Kansas closed that gap temporarily by treating the products as alcohol. Most states have not.

Why This Is an Enforcement Problem, Not a Labeling Problem

The issue is not simply whether ethanol is listed on a label.

The issue is whether it can be identified and regulated in real time.

  • No field test exists for ethanol content in these products at the point of sale
  • No consistent requirement exists to verify alcohol content before retail distribution
  • No uniform classification exists across states

That means even when standards exist on paper, they are difficult to enforce in practice.

Practical reality

If a regulator cannot identify a violation at the shelf, the regulation cannot function as intended.

What Kansas Changed

Kansas did not wait for a new category of regulation.

It applied an existing one.

By treating certain kratom liquid products as alcohol based on composition, the state enforced laws that already existed.

That approach avoided the need to redefine kratom entirely.

It focused on what the product actually is, not how it is marketed.

Bottom Line

Kansas showed:

  • Some kratom liquid products meet the definition of alcohol under state law

Independent lab data shows:

  • Ethanol levels exceeding accepted thresholds across multiple products

Taken together, the conclusion is straightforward.

These products are operating in a regulatory gap, and that gap is being used to avoid oversight.

This is not about isolated contamination.

It is about classification.

And classification determines whether a product is regulated at all.
