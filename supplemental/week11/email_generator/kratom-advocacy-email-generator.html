What Happens When You Put In Almost Nothing

The tool presents itself as a way for individuals to share their personal story.

So we tested that claim.

We entered a single placeholder:

X

No narrative. No details. No personal experience.

Then we generated the letter.

The Result

What came back was not a blank form.

It was a complete, structured advocacy letter.

  • A personal story was written for the user
  • A policy position was inserted
  • A regulatory framework was proposed
  • A technical argument about “natural vs synthetic” was included

All of that — from a single character input.

Key point

The system does not require a real story to produce a persuasive letter.

What This Means

The letter reads as if it came from an individual with lived experience.

But the substance of the message does not come from the user.

It comes from the system.

That distinction matters.

  • It is presented as constituent testimony
  • It is structured as policy argument
  • It appears individualized

But it is generated from a template.

The Consistency Problem

When one person submits a structured letter, it is testimony.

When many people submit nearly identical letters generated from minimal input, it becomes something else.

It becomes coordinated messaging presented as independent input.

The Narrative That Appears Regardless of Input

Even with minimal input, certain themes consistently appear in the generated output.

The most prominent is the distinction between “natural kratom” and “synthetic 7-hydroxymitragynine.”

This distinction is presented as central to the policy argument.

  • Natural kratom is framed as stable and traditional
  • Synthetic 7-OH is framed as the source of risk
  • Regulation is framed as the appropriate response

That framing appears even when the user provides no meaningful input.

What stands out

The argument is not built from the user’s experience.
It is inserted into the response.

Consistency Across Outputs

A single generated letter may look personal.

But the structure reveals something more consistent.

Across outputs, the same elements repeat:

  • A personal benefit statement
  • A warning against prohibition
  • A focus on synthetic products as the primary concern
  • A recommendation for regulation over restriction

That consistency does not come from independent authors.

It comes from the underlying template.

The Framing Effect

For lawmakers, the distinction matters.

Constituent input is valuable when it reflects independent perspectives.

But when a system produces uniform arguments regardless of input, it shapes the signal being received.

The result is not necessarily false.

But it is not purely organic either.

Why this matters

Policy decisions rely on understanding how people actually think and experience an issue.
Template-driven messaging can blur that picture.

The Larger Question

This is not about whether people support or oppose kratom.

It is about how information reaches lawmakers.

If a letter appears to be personal, but is generated from minimal input, then the source of the argument matters.

What Legislators Should Consider

Before weighing these messages, three questions are worth asking:

  1. Was this written by the constituent?
    Or generated from a structured template?
  2. Does it reflect individual experience?
    Or a standardized policy narrative?
  3. How many similar messages are being received?
    And how independent are they?

What This Is — and What It Isn’t

This tool does not create false statements.

It creates consistent ones.

It does not fabricate positions.

It standardizes them.

And it does not require a meaningful input to do it.

That is the point to understand.

The message may be real.
But the structure is not organic.

Final Point

Legislators rely on public input to understand real-world impact.

That system works when the input reflects independent voices.

It becomes less clear when identical arguments are produced at scale, with minimal input, and delivered one at a time.

This is not a claim about intent.

It is an observation about structure.

If the process is automated,
the appearance of grassroots support deserves a closer look.

At minimum, it raises a reasonable question:

Are you hearing from constituents,
or from a system designed to speak for them?
