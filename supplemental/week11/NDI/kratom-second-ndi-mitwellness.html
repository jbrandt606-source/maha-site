Week 11 FDA & NDI Kratom Extracts

Another Kratom NDI Stumbles — And This One Is Hard to Defend

Some submissions raise questions.

Others answer them before you even have to ask.

The latest kratom New Dietary Ingredient filing from TNT Manufacturing, d/b/a MitWellness, led by President Todd Underwood, does the latter.

It’s Not Raw Leaf. It’s a 75% Extract.

The ingredient is clearly defined as:

“Mitragyna speciosa leaf extract standardized to 75 ± 3.5% mitragynine.”

That is a highly concentrated, standardized extract.

At the same time, the safety argument leans heavily on traditional use, including tea and chewed leaf, where exposure is far lower and far less controlled.

That contrast is not subtle.

It is presented, then largely left unresolved.

FDA Had to Ask for the Basics

Before getting into the science, FDA flagged the submission for missing required materials.

  • Full-text studies were not included
  • Abstract-only citations were relied upon
  • Additional documentation was requested to complete the record

In other words:

The submission did not initially include all of the evidence it relied on.

That is not a disagreement over conclusions.

That is a gap in the submission itself.

For a safety submission, this matters.

FDA was not simply debating interpretation. It was asking for the record to be completed.

The Pharmacology Section Says More Than It Should

The document describes mitragynine as:

  • A partial µ-opioid receptor agonist
  • Active at serotonin, dopamine, and adrenergic receptors
  • Metabolized by major CYP drug pathways

It also advises users to consult healthcare professionals due to interaction risks.

That is all straightforward.

But it creates a problem the document does not solve:

The more clearly the pharmacology is described, the harder it becomes to treat the ingredient as routine.

The “Traditional Use” Bridge Never Quite Connects

The submission presents two very different exposure stories.

  • Traditional use: lower-dose preparations, roughly 50–150 mg per day
  • Proposed ingredient: a 75% standardized mitragynine extract

It acknowledges differences in formulation and pharmacokinetics.

But instead of resolving those differences, it moves past them.

That is where the argument starts to feel incomplete.

The Adverse Events Section Is Extensive — Then Qualified

The filing includes a wide range of case reports.

  • Liver injury
  • Neurologic effects
  • Respiratory complications
  • Renal injury
  • Death

It also emphasizes limitations.

  • Polysubstance use
  • Incomplete data
  • Uncertain causation

All fair.

But listing that many serious outcomes and then stepping back from them leaves a noticeable tension in the document.

Even Dependence Is Acknowledged

The submission states that:

  • Chronic use can lead to tolerance and dependence
  • Withdrawal symptoms may resemble those of opioids

That is not implied.

It is written directly into the filing.

This is where the “safe botanical” pitch runs into its own paperwork.

The submission itself acknowledges dependence and opioid-like withdrawal concerns.

What Makes This Awkward

None of these points require creative interpretation.

They are all in the document.

That makes the overall presentation difficult to reconcile:

  • A highly concentrated extract supported by lower-dose traditional use
  • A submission FDA had to ask to complete
  • A pharmacology section that reads like a drug profile
  • Safety signals that are presented but not fully resolved

It is not one issue.

It is how all of them sit next to each other.

Bottom Line

This submission does not collapse under a single flaw.

It struggles because the central argument never quite comes together.

A standardized, pharmacologically active extract is evaluated using a framework built for something less concentrated and less defined.

And that gap shows up repeatedly.
